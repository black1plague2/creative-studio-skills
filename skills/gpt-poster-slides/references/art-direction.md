# Poster-slide art direction

## Build a subject-specific identity

Describe what the visual language communicates about this topic. A campus borrowing concept can use lived campus scenes and objects; a cultural project can use an appropriate ornamental vocabulary; a scientific explanation may need a restrained visual evidence system. Do not select a theme solely because it is fashionable.

Record these commitments:

| Role | Decision to make | Review question |
|---|---|---|
| Palette | Dominant field, text, supporting colors, emphasis | Does color guide meaning and remain legible? |
| Typography | Display/body/labels; scale and density | Can exact text be read in the final image? |
| Illustration | Medium, stroke, texture, characters/objects | Is this specific to the subject and coherent? |
| Motifs | Repeatable useful shapes/scenes/patterns | Does the motif support content rather than decorate it? |
| Layout | Focal point, hierarchy, groupings, safe margins | Is the intended reading path apparent? |
| Technical grammar | Arrows, categories, stage labels, evidence treatment | Are relationships correct and understandable? |

## Four possible directions, not mandatory presets

**Sketchbook:** grid/paper ground, energetic marker strokes, hand-drawn device/people illustrations, clear black contours. Appropriate when the tone is playful and explanations are tangible. Technical panels must remain orderly even if strokes are lively.

**Cultural editorial:** warm or otherwise subject-appropriate colors, expressive type and a researched ornamental vocabulary. Use imagery and motifs respectfully and specifically; do not add arbitrary cultural symbols to unrelated subjects. Support product scenes and technical content with the same identity.

**Illustrated environmental/campus world:** cut-paper terrain, people, hardware and landscape motifs with strong display lettering. Connect visual detail to the mechanism or user journey. Keep labels/arrow direction precise in dense plates.

**Restrained illustrated narrative:** quiet dark/light fields, deliberate type contrast, sketch-like objects and repeated thematic metaphors. A timeline can use recurring meaningful objects rather than generic boxes. Quiet does not mean empty or interchangeable.

## Design by slide role

- **Cover:** one memorable scene and exact name/message. Establish the grammar.
- **Problem:** show a concrete situation and consequence. Numbers require sources.
- **Solution:** show what changes and for whom, not a row of abstract adjectives.
- **Architecture:** illustrate real components and faithful relationships. Keep names and constraints exact.
- **User flow:** show actors, objects, decisions and consequences in a readable sequence.
- **Evidence:** preserve measured figures and qualification; generation must not redraw data inaccurately.
- **Comparison:** align comparable categories, use strong visual difference, preserve nuance.
- **Roadmap:** show real milestones/proposals without implying shipped status.
- **Closing:** state the useful next step or conclusion appropriate to the audience.

## Density is chosen, not policed by one quota

The references that established this repository's direction contain both open cover/benefit compositions and richly detailed technical infographics. A dense plate can be successful when its grouping, hierarchy, labels and source relationships remain legible. A giant headline with decorative art can fail when it hides the required evidence.

Review at the intended use scale, and also at full size to catch errors. Reduce or split content before shrinking essential copy. Do not count a random icon as meaningful visual communication, and do not ban legitimate diagrams/cards/borders merely because another skill dislikes their default use.
