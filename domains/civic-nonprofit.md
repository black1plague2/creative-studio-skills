# Civic and nonprofit: dignity, eligibility and service truth

Use for public information, community initiatives, service concepts and nonprofit communications. Distinguish reported need, proposed action, accepted request and completed outcome. A campaign is not authority to promise delivery, collect sensitive stories or publish beneficiaries.

## Editable brief

- Community/audience, language and literacy needs: [fill].
- Intended action and responsible service/organization: [fill].
- Eligibility, geography, dates, contact and official source: [fill].
- Status of initiative and permission for identifiable stories/images: [fill].
- Any impact data, denominator and collection method: [fill].
- Artifact, low-bandwidth/print needs and alternate route: [fill].

Ask: Who is eligible and what action is actually available? What official source supports dates/contact/status? Which stories or images have permission, and can anonymized or fictional illustration meet the goal? Real contacts stay unresolved until supplied; do not invent helplines, agencies or donations routes.

## Creative constraints

Avoid pity-driven imagery and labels that reduce people to hardship. Use clear public-service language and accountable stages. A map needs actual geography; illustrative neighborhood art is not a location record. Keep event dates, eligibility and fallback route readable. No invented impact metrics, official seals, endorsements or guarantees of service completion.

## Filled fictional exercise — OpenBlock

Original maintenance-reporting prototype for adult neighborhood residents. Proposed flow: choose a damaged-footpath category → describe an approximate location → receive a report receipt. No municipal integration, verified address, emergency handling or repair commitment exists. Illustrations show a generic neighborhood with no identifiable residents. Exact headline: **“Make the issue clear.”** Receipt: **“Report received in this demo. No repair has been scheduled.”** The exercise includes no charity or government endorsement.

## Task adaptations

| Group | Domain-specific treatment |
|---|---|
| Brief and language | Explain report versus repair; use respectful wording and avoid implied official authority. |
| Slide and poster | GPT 16:9 illustrated posters show footpath, description and receipt; print signage separately requires actual location/contact facts and correct dimensions. |
| Reading documents | Guide organizes who/what/how/status; blank real eligibility/contact fields remain open, not filled with plausible guesses. |
| Website and app | Public page identifies demo status; app states include missing location, offline/save failure and submitted receipt. Do not treat this flow as an emergency channel. |
| Video, voice and interaction | A resident-focused explanation states receipt limits plainly; actually listen for a respectful, conversational tone. Clickable demo lets viewers describe a simulated issue and read the result. |
| Identity, assets and campaign | Original neighborhood scenes and readable civic hierarchy; social creatives keep demo status in crop and captions and show people with agency. |
| Data and evidence | Received reports are not completed repairs. Synthetic categories cannot become community impact statistics. |

Review failure example: “Report accepted” is shown with an animated crew fixing the road. It implies a delivered outcome unsupported by the prototype and requires narrative/visual correction.
