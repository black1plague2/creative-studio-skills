# Education: design the learner's next thought

Use for lessons, workshops, course material, learning products and educational outreach. Specify learner level, prior knowledge, the observable learning action and whether a quiz is practice or formal assessment. A polished lesson is not evidence of learning gain.

## Editable brief

- Learner group, age context, language and prerequisite knowledge: [fill].
- One observable objective: [what the learner can explain/do afterward].
- Supplied content, source and misconceptions to address: [fill].
- Duration and delivery: [self-paced, live, printed, LMS or web].
- Feedback/assessment intent: [practice versus graded; fill].
- Requested task and accessibility alternatives: [fill].

Ask material gaps: What should the learner be able to do? What do they already know, and which source supplies the explanation? Should responses be assessed, or should wrong answers simply guide another attempt? If learners are children, preserve the intended age/consent context without inventing student records.

## Creative constraints

Concrete examples precede abstract vocabulary where useful. Explain one actual mechanism rather than placing nouns in decorative boxes. Keep terminology and notation consistent. Feedback says why an answer fits or does not; avoid punishment or unexplained red crosses. Do not invent accreditation, examination alignment, completion rates or learning-outcome claims. Captioned media and readable alternatives support access; their presence does not itself certify accessibility.

## Filled fictional exercise — LanternLab

Original fictional scenario: a 20-minute adult digital-literacy workshop on checking a suspicious message. Objective: identify a mismatched sender address and choose to inspect the destination before clicking. The exercise uses a clearly simulated sender and link, never a live malicious URL. The supplied scenario defines warning cues but does not teach a complete security certification. Exact copy: **“Pause. Check the sender. Inspect the link.”** Learners practice two choices with explanation and retry. No completion or fraud-prevention rate is supplied.

## Task adaptations

| Group | Domain-specific treatment |
|---|---|
| Brief and language | Turn objective into a concrete learner action; avoid “master cybersecurity” for a short practice activity. |
| Slide and poster | Full GPT 16:9 scene posters can show message inspection with large callouts; preserve exact suspicious-address examples and mark simulation. A print poster needs its own physical geometry. |
| Reading documents | Instructor sheet separates objective, demo, activity and answer rationale; learner PDF uses short instructions and visible example context. |
| Website and app | Course page makes scope clear; activity states include attempted, feedback, retry and completed practice. A correct answer is not a professional qualification. |
| Video, voice and interaction | Voice sounds like an instructor speaking to one learner; actual listening checks terms and pauses. Interactive choice changes the explanation and allows retry; linear MP4 is the noninteractive fallback. |
| Identity, assets and campaign | Warm desk/message illustration with purposeful annotation; portfolio/social material names a practice workshop and never an invented school partnership. |
| Data and evidence | No attainment chart without a defined measure and real dataset. Sample answer records stay synthetic and have no learner identity. |

Review failure example: both choices lead to the same generic “Great job!” response. It fails meaningful feedback even if the buttons work; repair the consequence and rationale before polishing animation.
